# Anti-Defensive Business Writing

## Core principle

Treat a business plan as an investment case, not a project log, product manual, technology report, or self-critique.

Identify the project's strongest credible commercial value and organize the entire narrative around it. Make every section, paragraph, table, and claim strengthen the same decision:

> This problem matters, this solution is meaningfully differentiated, the business can work, and this team has a credible path to execute.

Do not optimize for "showing how much work was done." Optimize for "making the strongest claim that the evidence can honestly support."

## Non-negotiable truth boundary

Never invent or upgrade evidence.

Do not fabricate or misrepresent:
- users, customers, orders, revenue, contracts, LOIs, pilots, partnerships, or awards;
- patents, technical performance, market share, certifications, policies, or research data;
- survey results, conversion rates, unit economics, financial forecasts, or competitive facts.

Do not describe an intention as a signed agreement, a prototype as a deployed product, a free pilot as paid validation, or a projection as historical revenue.

Anti-defensive writing means controlling the scope of the claim, not hiding material facts. Disclose risks or limitations when they materially affect the commercial conclusion, but state them precisely and proportionately.

## Workflow

### 1. Find the real win condition

Before rewriting, determine the strongest defensible advantage. Look for one or more of:
- a painful and valuable problem;
- a neglected or poorly served customer segment;
- lower cost, faster delivery, higher efficiency, or lower deployment friction;
- a capability unavailable in incumbent solutions;
- stronger fit for a constrained or high-value scenario;
- proprietary data, workflow integration, technical mechanism, channel, or supply advantage;
- a credible business-model advantage;
- better scalability, repeatability, or unit economics;
- a timing advantage created by policy, technology, behavior, or industry change.

Do not make a weak metric the main battlefield. If the project cannot credibly win on market size, model accuracy, feature count, brand, funding, user count, or raw performance, redefine the comparison around the dimension that actually matters to the target customer.

### 2. State the core proposition in one sentence

Build a sentence in this form:

> For [target customer] facing [high-value problem], the project provides [distinct solution] through [key mechanism], creating [measurable value] and enabling [commercial outcome].

Use this sentence as the control point for the rest of the plan.

### 3. Rebuild the story around commercial logic

Prefer this causal order:

1. Problem and loss
2. Why existing solutions are insufficient
3. Market opportunity and timing
4. Product/solution
5. Differentiated capability and moat
6. Evidence and validation
7. Business model
8. Go-to-market and growth path
9. Financial logic
10. Team and execution credibility
11. Milestones, funding/resource use, and key risks

Do not organize the story chronologically as "first we researched, then we built, then we tested, then we improved."

### 4. Translate technology into business value

Never stop at a technology label.

Translate each important technical claim through this chain:

> technology/mechanism -> product capability -> user benefit -> economic value -> commercial advantage

Bad:
> We use multimodal AI and digital twins.

Better:
> Multimodal perception reduces manual review in complex scenarios, shortening decision time and lowering service-delivery cost; this makes deployment economically viable for the target customer group.

If a technical feature cannot be linked to user value or moat, reduce its prominence.

### 5. Use evidence as argument, not decoration

Every important number must have a job:
- market data proves opportunity;
- interviews/surveys prove the problem;
- tests prove product capability;
- usage proves demand;
- paid orders prove willingness to pay;
- retention/repurchase proves durable value;
- cost and margin data prove business viability;
- pilot replication proves scalability.

Prefer the strongest available evidence. A real paid customer can be more persuasive than a large generic survey.

Keep the claim no broader than the evidence.

### 6. Control comparison scope

Choose competitive dimensions that are both:
1. important to customer purchase decisions; and
2. supported by the project's evidence.

Useful dimensions may include:
- deployment cost;
- implementation time;
- scenario fit;
- automation level;
- learning curve;
- workflow integration;
- data-loop capability;
- service depth;
- total cost of ownership;
- scalability;
- monetization model.

Do not create a comparison table where the project "wins everything." A credible, selective comparison is stronger than universal superiority.

### 7. Make the business model concrete

Answer explicitly:
- Who pays?
- What do they pay for?
- How much and how often?
- What does delivery cost?
- What drives gross margin?
- What creates repeat purchase or retention?
- What allows scale?

Prefer one clear primary revenue engine over many speculative revenue streams.

### 8. Make market analysis reachable

Do not use a huge TAM as proof of success.

Separate:
- TAM: total theoretical market;
- SAM: market the product can actually serve;
- SOM: realistically obtainable market in the next 1-3 years.

The key question is not "How big is the industry?" but "Where do the first customers come from, and how does the project expand from there?"

### 9. Make finance traceable

Financial forecasts must map to business assumptions.

Prefer formulas such as:
- customers x average contract value = revenue;
- revenue - direct delivery cost = gross profit;
- sales/marketing spend / new customers = acquisition cost;
- recurring revenue / churn = retention economics.

Reject unsupported exponential growth curves. Explain the mechanism behind each growth step.

### 10. Present the team as execution fit

Do not turn the team section into a list of certificates and awards.

Map project needs to team capability:
- technology;
- product;
- industry knowledge;
- sales/channel access;
- operations;
- supply chain;
- finance/compliance;
- partnerships.

The section should answer: "Why is this team unusually well positioned to execute this plan?"

## Language rules

### Prefer assertive but bounded claims

Replace vague self-weakening language such as:
- "the project is still immature";
- "the team has limited capability";
- "there are many shortcomings";
- "the business model is not yet complete";
- "market recognition is relatively low".

Use stage-appropriate, evidence-bounded language instead:
- "The core product capability has been validated; the next stage focuses on commercial validation."
- "The current product covers the highest-value use case; adjacent scenarios are scheduled for later expansion."
- "Commercialization currently prioritizes initial reference customers before standardized replication."

Do not conceal material uncertainty. State it with the narrowest accurate scope and pair it with mitigation or the next validation milestone.

### Avoid empty prestige language

Reduce or delete:
- "empowering the industry";
- "building an ecosystem";
- "leading the transformation";
- "creating a new benchmark";
- "filling an international gap";
- "world-leading";
- "industry-leading";

unless the evidence can support the claim.

Prefer concrete nouns, verbs, mechanisms, numbers, customers, costs, and outcomes.

### Avoid generic openings

Do not begin with:
> With the rapid development of the economy / AI / big data / digitalization...

Open with the business contradiction:
> [Customer] currently loses [time/cost/revenue/risk] because [specific problem]. Existing solutions rely on [limitation], leaving [commercial gap].

## Default business-plan structure

Use this structure as a default, not a rigid template:

1. Executive summary / project overview
2. Problem and customer pain
3. Market opportunity and timing
4. Product and solution
5. Core technology / key capability / moat
6. Competitive landscape
7. Business model
8. Go-to-market and growth strategy
9. Validation and traction
10. Financial model and forecast
11. Team
12. Development milestones
13. Funding/resource plan
14. Key risks and mitigation

Reorder or merge sections when the evidence supports a stronger story.

## Competition-specific review

When the material is for an entrepreneurship or innovation competition, review it through four lenses:

- Technical judge: What is actually innovative?
- Commercial judge: Who buys and why?
- Investor judge: Can it scale and produce attractive economics?
- Industry judge: Can it be deployed in the real world?

The final plan should make all four answers easy to find.

## Adversarial review

Before finalizing, challenge the draft:

- Is the core commercial advantage obvious within the first pages?
- Is any claim broader than the evidence?
- Does any page exist only to show workload?
- Is technology being mistaken for value?
- Does market analysis prove access, not only size?
- Does the plan explain who pays and why?
- Is the project competing on a dimension where it is actually weak?
- Are speculative intentions being presented as traction?
- Can each financial forecast be traced to an assumption?
- Does the competitive analysis look credible rather than self-scored?
- Does the team section prove execution fit?
- Are material risks disclosed without turning the section into self-denial?

If deleting a page would not weaken the reader's reason to support the project, consider deleting it.

## Output behavior

When asked to review a business plan:
1. Identify the core commercial proposition and strongest evidence.
2. Identify the current story's biggest narrative mistakes.
3. Propose a stronger structure or positioning when necessary.
4. Rewrite the requested sections, preserving facts and figures unless the user authorizes new research.
5. Flag claims that need evidence rather than inventing support.

When asked to rewrite the whole plan, do not merely polish sentence-by-sentence. Reconstruct the story if that produces a stronger evidence-aligned case.

When asked only for wording improvements, preserve the original structure unless it materially undermines the stated goal.
